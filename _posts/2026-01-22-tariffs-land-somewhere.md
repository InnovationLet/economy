When a 10% tariff hits steel from overseas, the steel doesn't float through customs and stay the same price. In 2018, after tariffs went into effect, U.S. steel prices jumped by more than 30% in just months. That jump didn't vanish. Someone paid for it.

## The Price Pass-Through

Tariffs are taxes on imports. Companies that bring goods into the country pay the tax to Customs, but they don't usually eat the full cost. They pass most of it along. When that imported steel goes into making cars, washing machines, or building materials, the extra cost follows. Manufacturers raise prices to cover what they paid at the border. Retailers then raise prices to cover what they paid to manufacturers.

This pass-through isn't perfect. Sometimes companies absorb a small portion for a while, especially if they're worried about losing customers. But over time, the cost lands. Economists who studied the 2018 tariffs found that American consumers bore most of the burden. The tariffs were meant to help domestic producers, but households paid higher prices on many everyday items.

## Who Really Pays

It's tempting to think foreign exporters pay tariffs. They don't. The tax is paid when goods enter the U.S. by the importer, usually an American company. Exporters might lower their prices slightly to stay competitive, but they don't cover the full tax. The burden shifts to U.S. businesses and ultimately to U.S. buyers.

For some goods, like luxury items with few substitutes, companies can pass through almost all of the cost. For goods with competition, pass-through might be partial. But in most cases, prices go up. That's why tariffs rarely "get absorbed" by other countries. They land on someone here.

## The Uneven Impact

The cost isn't spread evenly. Lower income households spend more of their budgets on things like clothes, appliances, and food products that often have imported components. When prices rise on those goods, those families feel it more. A 5% price increase on a basic necessity hits harder than the same increase on something discretionary.

Also, some industries get protection while others pay more for inputs. A steel tariff helps steelmakers, but it hurts auto manufacturers who need steel. That creates a ripple effect across the economy.

## The Practical Takeaway

Before assuming tariffs help "us" without cost, look at your receipts. If prices are rising on goods you buy regularly, some of those increases might trace back to trade policy. Don't just watch what politicians say about tariffs; watch what you pay at the register. That's where the cost lands.
